1. ONE-PARAGRAPH SUMMARY
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

New York City publishes building data from several agencies, and each agency
identifies buildings in its own way. This project downloads eight of those
datasets for Manhattan, matches every record to a physical building and a tax
lot, and organises the result as an ontology: landlords, managing agents and
owners control lots; lots hold buildings; events (violations, complaints,
facade filings) happen at them. On top of that ontology it computes features
"as of" any date, trains an explainable model that predicts two kinds of
trouble in the next 12 months, tests the model on a year it never saw, and
serves the result in a Streamlit app where an inspection planner can see why a
building is risky and create, assign and close inspection tasks. A static
landing page on Vercel presents the project, and a browser version of the
tool is hosted next to it at /app/.

Headline numbers:
  - 1,281,497 events from 8 sources, Manhattan, 2020 onward
  - 95.2-99.7% of records matched to a tax lot, 89.2-100% to a building
  - 27,832 lots linked into 13,120 landlord portfolios (largest: 234 lots)
  - Held-out 2024 test, top 100 lots:
      91% had 10+ new class C housing violations (base rate 3.6%, about 25x)
      32% had a serious hazard (base rate 1.6%, about 20x)


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
2. THE PROBLEM AND WHO IT IS FOR
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

User: an inspection planner at a city agency (HPD or DOB), or a large property
manager reviewing a portfolio.

Decision supported: which buildings to inspect first this week, and why.

Why it is hard:
  - Evidence about deteriorating buildings exists, but it is split across
    agencies. HPD housing violations are keyed by block and lot. DOB
    violations, complaints and facade filings are keyed by building number
    (BIN). Ownership lives in PLUTO, a tax-lot file with no BIN at all.
  - The "owner" in PLUTO is usually a single-building LLC, which hides the
    landlord who actually controls dozens of buildings.
  - Raw counts mislead: a big building collects more violations simply by
    having more apartments, and some violations are paperwork, not danger.

What the project delivers:
  - One linked model of the data (the ontology).
  - A ranked list of lots with a probability for each risk and plain-language
    reasons ("facade UNSAFE; 26 open DOB violations; ...").
  - Honest evidence of how well the ranking works on a year it never saw.
  - A tool to act on it, with outcomes written back.

3. GLOSSARY
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

BBL      Borough-Block-Lot. The 10-digit tax-lot id: 1 digit borough
         (1 = Manhattan), 5 digits block, 4 digits lot. Example 1020730001.
BIN      Building Identification Number. 7 digits, first digit is the
         borough. Identifies a physical structure. One lot can hold several
         buildings.
"Million BIN"  Placeholder BINs like 1000000, 2000000 used when the real BIN
         is unknown. They must be ignored, never matched.
Condo billing lot  Condominium buildings appear in PLUTO once, under a billing
         lot numbered 75xx. Individual condo units have their own lots
         (1001+). Other datasets sometimes use the unit lot.
PLUTO    Primary Land Use Tax Lot Output, published by City Planning (DCP).
         One row per tax lot: address, year built, floors, units, owner.
DOB      Department of Buildings: construction, boilers, elevators, facades.
HPD      Department of Housing Preservation and Development: housing
         maintenance code (heat, leaks, lead paint, pests...).
HPD violation classes
         A  non-hazardous            (90 days to correct)
         B  hazardous                (30 days)
         C  immediately hazardous    (24 hours for most; 5 days for some)
         I  information orders
FISP     Facade Inspection Safety Program (Local Law 11). Buildings over six
         storeys file a facade report every five-year cycle. Statuses:
         SAFE, SWARMP (Safe With A Repair and Maintenance Program, meaning
         it needs repairs) and UNSAFE (hazardous, must be fixed right away).
HPD registration  Owners of residential rentals with 3+ units must register
         every year, naming the owner, head officer and managing agent.
Socrata  The platform behind NYC Open Data; its API supports SQL-like
         filters ($where, $select, $limit).
Ontology Here: a model of the domain as object types (Lot, Building...),
         link types between them, properties, and actions that change data.
         This mirrors Palantir Foundry's Ontology.
Precision@k  Of the k highest-ranked lots, the share that actually had the
         outcome. P@100 = 0.91 means 91 of the top 100.
Recall@k Of all lots that had the outcome, the share found in the top k.
Average precision (AP)  Summary of precision across the whole ranking
         (area under the precision-recall curve). Random ranking scores the
         base rate.
Lift     Precision divided by the base rate. 25x means 25 times better than
         picking lots at random.
Base rate  Share of all lots that had the outcome.
Point-in-time  Computing a feature using only data available on a given date,
         so a backtest cannot see the future.

Filters used:
  PLUTO              borough = 'MN'
  Footprints         1000000 <= bin < 2000000 (Manhattan BINs)
  DOB violations     boro = '1' and issue_date >= 20200101
  HPD violations     boroid = '1' and inspection date >= 2020-01-01

  Facade filings     borough = 'Manhattan' (all cycles)
  Registrations      boroid = '1'

                     cannot filter contacts by borough; they are joined to

Why these sources: together they cover the three things a planner needs:
what the building is (PLUTO, footprints), what has gone wrong there (four
event feeds), and who is responsible (registrations).

Left out on purpose (to fit a 10-12 hour budget): 311 service requests
(23M rows citywide, address-only matching) and FDNY incidents (6M rows).

A data note: the DOB complaints feed first appeared to stop on 2024-04-15.
DOB refreshed the dataset on 2026-10-25 and the same query then returned
174,695 rows instead of 110,772, running to 2026-10-26. Re-downloading fixed
the gap; no separate "DOB NOW complaints" dataset exists.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
5. DOWNLOADING THE DATA        (scripts/download.sh)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

A bash script with one helper, get(name, id, where, [select]), which calls
the Socrata CSV endpoint with $where, optional $select and $limit=5000000,
retries up to three times on any error (including TLS resets), and prints
row count and size. Output: data/raw/<name>.csv, about 480 MB in total.
data/ is git-ignored; anyone can rebuild it by re-running the script.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
6. ENTITY RESOLUTION        (scripts/build_db.py)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Goal: every event record gets a building (BIN) and a tax lot (BBL), whatever
key its source uses.

Reference tables built first:
  lot       from PLUTO; BBL normalised to a 10-digit string.
  building  from footprints; keeps only real Manhattan BINs (7 digits,
            starting with 1, not a million-BIN). Each building carries
            mappluto_bbl (the PLUTO lot it belongs to, which is the condo
            billing lot for condos) and base_bbl (its own lot).

Each source's block and lot are turned into a BBL with
'1' + block padded to 5 digits + lot padded to 4 digits, only when both are
positive integers.

Resolution order for every record (the match_method column records which
step won):
  1. bin         The record's BIN is a real building in footprints.
                 BBL = that building's mappluto_bbl.
  2. bbl         The record's block/lot is a PLUTO lot. If that lot holds
                 exactly one building, its BIN is filled in too.
  3. condo_unit  The block/lot is a condo unit lot (found as some building's
                 base_bbl); it is mapped to the PLUTO billing lot.
  4. bin_only    The BIN looks real but is not in footprints (demolished or
                 newly built). BIN kept, BBL left empty.
  5. unresolved  Nothing usable.

Results (table match_report):

  Source          Rows      Has BBL  Has BIN  by_bin   by_bbl  condo  bin_only unresolved
  dob_complaint   174,695    95.2%   100.0%   166,282       0      0    8,413        0
  dob_violation   100,769    99.5%   100.0%    97,809   2,179    280      492        9
  facade_filing    50,979    99.6%    99.9%    50,159     508    111      186       15
  hpd_violation   955,054    99.7%    89.2%         0 951,856    574        0    2,624

Why HPD reaches only 89% for BIN: HPD records carry block/lot only. When a
lot holds several buildings, there is no way to tell which one a violation
belongs to, so BIN stays empty rather than guessed.

Data quality handled here:
  - Placeholder BINs (1000000 etc.) never match.
  - Junk dates (e.g. 'Y9990120') parse to NULL via try_strptime.
  - Date formats differ by source: YYYYMMDD (DOB violations), ISO
    timestamps (HPD), MM/DD/YYYY (complaints, facades). Each is parsed
    explicitly.
  - Fixed-width DOB violation type strings are split into a code
    ("LBLVIO") and a description.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The ontology is defined in ontology/ontology.yaml and materialised as DuckDB
tables. It deliberately mirrors Palantir Foundry's concepts: object types
backed by tables, link types as keys, actions as writeback.

  ManagingAgent       10,436  Management company or person from the latest
                              HPD registration. Key agent_id.
  Owner               32,871  Owner of record from PLUTO, name-normalised.
                              Key owner_id. Properties: name, owner_type

8. CLEANING AND CLASSIFICATION DECISIONS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

8.1 Event severity (0-3), used across sources:
      HPD:      C = 3, B = 2, A and I = 1
      DOB:      hazard = 3, admin = 1, equipment / construction / other = 2
      Complaint: 1
      Facade:   UNSAFE = 3, SWARMP = 2, No Report Filed = 2, SAFE = 0

8.2 DOB violations are not all risks. Every type code was classified:
      hazard        FISPHAZ (facade hazard), FISPFCS (failure to correct
                    facade repairs), IMEGNCY, EGNCY (emergencies), COMPBLD
                    (structurally compromised), UB (unsafe building), CLOS
                    (padlock)                                    3,947 rows
      equipment     boilers (LBLVIO, HBLVIO, B, LL6291), elevators (E,
                    AEUHAZ1, EVCAT5, HVCAT5, JVCAT5, LL1081)     69,913 rows
      construction  C, P                                          7,140 rows
      admin         BENCH (failure to benchmark energy), EGRADE (energy
                    grade not posted), EARCX (energy report), LANDMK, Z,
                    FISPNRF (facade report not filed), FISP, RWNRF
                                                                 19,769 rows
    About a fifth of DOB violations are paperwork. Without this split a
    building that forgot to post its energy grade would look dangerous.

8.3 Open at a past date. A violation counts as open on date D if it closed
    on or after D, or (no close date known) it is open now. HPD has no
    explicit close date; the current-status date is used when the
    violation is closed (an approximation).

8.4 Owner name normalisation: uppercase, punctuation removed, spaces
    collapsed, trailing entity suffixes stripped (LLC, L L C, INC, CORP,
    CORPORATION, CO, LP, L P, LTD, LLP, COMPANY). Placeholders such as
    "UNAVAILABLE OWNER" and "NAME NOT ON FILE" get no owner.
    owner_id = 'own_' + first 12 hex digits of md5(name).

8.5 Owner type from PLUTO's ownertype code: C city, M mixed, O other public,
    X tax-exempt, P or blank private (blank is overwhelmingly private).

9. LANDLORD PORTFOLIOS AND MANAGING AGENTS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Problem: PLUTO's owner of record is usually an LLC that owns one building
("BIRDIE 141 BROADWAY ASSOCIATES"). The landlord behind it may control many.

Method (the approach used by tools such as JustFix "Who Owns What"):
  1. Each Manhattan HPD registration is mapped to a PLUTO lot (condo unit
     lots mapped to the billing lot, as in section 6).
  2. From its contacts, the registration contributes linking keys:
       person keys   "P:FIRST LAST" for HeadOfficer, IndividualOwner and
                     JointOwner contacts (first name present, last name
                     longer than one character)
       company keys  "C:NAME" for CorporateOwner names longer than three
                     characters
  3. A graph connects lots to keys. Its connected components, found with a
     union-find, are the portfolios.
  4. Each portfolio is named after its most-shared person (else company).
     portfolio_id = 'pf_' + md5 of the component root.

Managing agents are a separate object and are NOT used for linking: one
management company (e.g. FirstService Residential, 349 lots) runs buildings
for many unrelated owners, and linking through it would merge strangers.
Each lot gets the agent from its most recent registration
(company name, else person name).

Result: 27,832 lots in 13,120 portfolios. 10,174 portfolios are a single
lot; the largest has 234 lots. A sanity check before committing to the
method showed no runaway merge (top sizes 236, 231, 169, ...), and known
large Manhattan landlords appear among the largest. The method even merged
spelling variants that appear on the same registration ("STEVEN CROMAN" and
"STEVE CROMAN").

Worked example (the #1 lot today, 3458 Broadway):
  Owner of record: BIRDIE 141 BROADWAY ASSOCIATES (one building)
  -> HPD registration head officer
  -> landlord portfolio of 59 lots, 5,086 units
  -> 41 of the other 58 lots are in Manhattan's riskiest 10% (median
     percentile 94.5; 9 are in the top 2%).

10. POINT-IN-TIME FEATURES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Two DuckDB table macros compute features as of any date, counting only
events strictly before that date:

    select * from lot_features_at(DATE '2024-01-01');
    select * from building_features_at(DATE '2024-01-01');

This one design choice makes the backtest honest: the same code produces
training features (as of 2023-01-01), test features (as of 2024-01-01) and
today's live features, and none can see later events. Each call runs in
under half a second.

lot_features_at(as_of) returns, per lot:

  Building facts
    building_age            as_of year minus year built
    num_floors, units_res, units_total, num_buildings, is_condo,
    is_landmark, in_hist_district

  HPD (housing)
    hpd_c_12m, hpd_b_12m, hpd_a_12m   new class C / B / A+I, last 12 months
    hpd_open_c                        class C still open on as_of
    hpd_rent_impairing_12m            rent-impairing violations, 12 months
    hpd_bc_last_6m, hpd_bc_prior_6m   B+C in the last 6 months vs the 6
                                      before (trend)
    hpd_c_per_100_units               class C per 100 units, 12 months

  DOB
    dob_hazard_36m                    hazard-class violations, 36 months
    dob_equipment_12m                 boiler / elevator, 12 months
    dob_admin_12m                     paperwork, 12 months
    dob_open                          non-admin violations open on as_of
    complaints_12m                    DOB complaints, 12 months

  Facade
    facade_status                     worst latest status across the lot's
                                      buildings (latest filing per BIN)
    facade_severity                   0-3
    facade_filing_age_years           age of the oldest "latest filing"

  Owner, landlord, agent (rates over all of their lots, this one included)
    owner_lots, owner_c_per_100_units
    portfolio_lots, portfolio_c_per_100_units
    portfolio_other_hazards_36m       hazard violations on the landlord's
                                      OTHER buildings
    portfolio_other_unsafe_facades    UNSAFE facades on the landlord's
                                      OTHER buildings
    agent_lots, agent_c_per_100_units
  These are NULL when the lot has no such link, rather than lumping every
  unlinked lot into one "unknown" group (a bug that was found and fixed).

building_features_at(as_of) gives the same event counts keyed on BIN, for
drilling into a single building.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
11. OUTCOMES (WHAT THE MODEL PREDICTS)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Two outcomes, each over the 12 months after the score date:

  serious_hazard     at least one DOB hazard-class violation OR a facade
                     filing with status UNSAFE.
                     2024 base rate: 661 of 40,507 lots = 1.63%
  housing_distress   10 or more new HPD class C violations.
                     2024 base rate: 1,459 of 40,507 lots = 3.60%

Universe: the 40,507 lots that have at least one building (vacant lots are
excluded; counting over all 42,504 lots gives 671 and 1,460).

Why two outcomes rather than one "risk": they behave differently. Housing
distress is chronic and predictable from recent class C counts. Serious
hazards are rarer and driven by facades and repeat hazards. Section 12
shows that blending them into one score made it worse at both.

Important caveat: outcomes are RECORDED violations. They partly reflect
where agencies choose to inspect. The score predicts "will get a serious
violation", not "is unsafe".


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
12. THE MODELS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

12.1 Components. Every model except gradient boosting works on 0-1
     "components" so that coefficients are comparable and explainable.
     Counts are log-scaled and capped: saturate(x) = log(1+x) / log(1+p99),
     clipped to [0, 1], where p99 is the 99th percentile of the non-zero
     values on that date.

       open_c             saturate(hpd_open_c)
       c_count            saturate(hpd_c_12m)
       c_rate             saturate(100 * hpd_c_12m / (units_res + 10))
                          (the +10 shrinks small buildings toward zero, so
                          an 8-unit building with a few violations does not
                          look extreme)
       dob_hazard         saturate(dob_hazard_36m)
       facade             facade_severity / 3            (v1 only)
       facade_unsafe      1 if latest facade status UNSAFE
       facade_swarmp      1 if SWARMP or no report filed
       dob_open           saturate(dob_open)
       equipment          saturate(dob_equipment_12m)
       trend              saturate(hpd_bc_last_6m - hpd_bc_prior_6m)
       complaints         saturate(complaints_12m)
       age                (building_age - 20) / 100, clipped to [0, 1]
       hazard_any         1 if any DOB hazard in 36 months
       unsafe_and_hazard  1 if UNSAFE facade AND a past hazard
       portfolio_c_rate   saturate(portfolio_c_per_100_units)
       portfolio_hazards  saturate(portfolio_other_hazards_36m)
       portfolio_unsafe   saturate(portfolio_other_unsafe_facades)
       agent_c_rate       saturate(agent_c_per_100_units)

12.2 rules_v1 (hand-set weights, set before looking at any outcome).
     Score = 100 x weighted sum: open_c 20%, dob_hazard 20%, facade 15%,
     c_rate 15%, dob_open 10%, equipment 5%, trend 5%, complaints 5%,
     age 5%. One score for both outcomes. It lost to simple baselines
     (section 14) and was kept only as a lesson.

12.3 learned_v2. One logistic regression PER OUTCOME (scikit-learn,
     C = 1.0) over ten components: open_c, c_count, c_rate, dob_hazard,
     facade_unsafe, facade_swarmp, dob_open, equipment, trend, age.
     Trained on features as of 2023-01-01 and 2023 outcomes. The
     coefficients are the weights, so the model stays explainable.

12.4 learned_v3 (the live model). v2 plus, per outcome, the additions that
     won on the validation split (section 13):
       serious_hazard    v2 + hazard_any, unsafe_and_hazard
       housing_distress  v2 + portfolio_c_rate, portfolio_hazards,
                         portfolio_unsafe, agent_c_rate

     v3 coefficients (fit on 2023):

       Component          serious_hazard   housing_distress
       open_c                 +0.55            +3.34
       c_count                +0.18            +4.59
       c_rate                 -0.02            -3.07
       dob_hazard             -0.27            -0.10
       facade_unsafe          +2.68            +0.35
       facade_swarmp          +1.93            -0.05
       dob_open               +1.69            +0.19
       equipment              +0.41            +0.47
       trend                  +0.03            +0.34
       age                    -0.14            -0.14
       hazard_any             -0.32              -
       unsafe_and_hazard      +1.15              -
       portfolio_c_rate         -              +1.56
       portfolio_hazards        -              +0.72
       portfolio_unsafe         -              +0.04
       agent_c_rate             -              +1.45

     Reading them: hazards are driven by facade status (UNSAFE most, then
     SWARMP), open DOB violations and the UNSAFE-plus-history combination.
     Housing distress is driven by class C counts, open class C, and the
     landlord's and agent's class C rates. Negative weights on correlated
     components (c_rate, dob_hazard, hazard_any) are collinearity: their
     signal is already carried by a related component.

12.5 Comparisons in every backtest:
       gradient boosting   scikit-learn HistGradientBoostingClassifier
                           (300 iterations, learning rate 0.05) on the raw
                           features. An accuracy ceiling; not explainable.
       most violations     total violations and complaints, last 12 months
       same signal         hazards: dob_hazard_36m + 10 x facade_severity;
                           distress: hpd_c_12m (last year's class C)
       oldest building     building age
       random


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
13. EVALUATION METHOD: TRAIN, VALIDATE, TEST
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Three time-based stages, so the test year is never used for a decision:

  Validation (choose inputs)  fit on features as of 2022-01-01 and 2022
                              outcomes; score as of 2023-01-01; compare
                              against 2023 outcomes.
  Training                    fit the chosen models on 2023-01-01 features
                              and 2023 outcomes.
  Test (run once)             score as of 2024-01-01 using only earlier
                              events; compare against 2024 outcomes.

Splits are by time, never random, because a random split would let a model
learn from a building's future to predict its past.

Validation table (fit 2022, scored on 2023; AP / P@100):

  Added to v2            serious_hazard       housing_distress
  (nothing, v2)          0.104 / 23%          0.510 / 90%
  +complaints            0.100 / 19%          0.510 / 85%
  +repeat offender       0.115 / 35%          0.510 / 90%
  +landlord & agent      0.099 / 24%          0.531 / 92%
  +all                   0.106 / 28%          0.530 / 91%

Decision: repeat-offender components for hazards, landlord and agent
components for distress. Complaints helped neither and were left out.

Caveat: features as of 2022-01-01 see only 24 of the 36 months for 3-year
windows, because the data starts in 2020.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
15. WHAT THE RESULTS MEAN
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

1. The hand-weighted score lost. Weights chosen by intuition, and one score
   for two different outcomes, did worse than "last year's count". Fitting
   one model per outcome fixed it while staying explainable.

2. Explainability costs nothing for housing distress. The logistic model
   matches gradient boosting at the top (91% vs 91%) and slightly beats it
   overall (AP 0.572 vs 0.570).

3. Landlords matter. The landlord's and managing agent's class C rates were
   chosen on validation and held up on the test: AP 0.563 -> 0.572.

4. Validation gains do not always replicate. Repeat-offender components
   lifted hazard P@100 from 23% to 35% on validation, but on the test v3 got
   32% vs v2's 34% (AP 0.143 -> 0.146). With about 660 positives, P@100 has
   roughly +/-5 points of noise (binomial standard error at p = 0.33 and
   n = 100 is about 4.7 points). No further tuning was done on the test
   year, because that would quietly turn it into training data.

5. For hazards, a naive repeat-offender rule ("had a hazard or unsafe facade
   before") leads in the top 100 (50%). Buildings with unsafe facades keep
   getting cited. The models are better over the whole ranking (AP), not at
   the very top. A real deployment should put known repeat offenders first
   explicitly and use the model to rank everything after them.

6. Age alone is useless (0% in the top 100): old buildings are common, and
   age without condition data predicts nothing.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
16. FROM SCORE TO DRIVERS, PERCENTILE AND RANK
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

For each lot the live model produces:
  p_serious_hazard, p_housing_distress   probabilities over 12 months
  score        100 x the larger of the two percentile ranks
               ("worse than X% of Manhattan lots on its worse risk")
  primary_risk the outcome with the higher percentile
  rank         1 = worst. Many lots tie near 100, so ties are broken by the
               other outcome's percentile.
  drivers      the three largest positive contributions (coefficient x
               component value) for the primary outcome, written in plain
               words, e.g.
               "facade UNSAFE; 26 open DOB violations; UNSAFE facade with
               past hazard violations"

Today's top 100 splits 50 / 50 between the two risks.

Scores are written to risk_assessment for two dates: 2024-01-01 (the
backtest) and the run date (live).

18. WRITEBACK AND STORAGE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Tasks live in data/actions.duckdb, separate from data/building_risk.duckdb.
The app opens actions.duckdb read-write and attaches the risk database
read-only as "br". Reason: build_db.py deletes and recreates the risk
database, and recorded inspector work must never be lost in a rebuild.

Consequence: stop the app before re-running the build scripts, because
DuckDB lets only one process write a file and the app holds a lock on it.

Recorded outcomes (hazard_found, minor_issues, no_issue, no_access) are the
labels a future model version would learn from: the feedback loop.

22. DESIGN DECISIONS AND TRADE-OFFS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Lot (BBL) as the scoring unit, not building (BIN)
  Every source reaches the lot (99.5%+); HPD cannot reach the building when
  a lot has several. Most Manhattan lots hold one building (37,924 of
  42,504), so little is lost, and Building 360 still drills to BIN.

DuckDB instead of Postgres or a graph database
  One file, no server, fast columnar SQL, table macros for point-in-time
  features. A graph database was unnecessary: the links are simple keys,
  and the one graph problem (portfolios) is a union-find in Python.

Logistic regression over gradient boosting for the product
  Coefficients are readable weights and every score splits into named
  drivers. Gradient boosting is kept as a ceiling to measure what
  explainability costs (nothing for distress; real for hazards).

Two outcomes instead of one risk score
  They have different drivers and base rates; one blended score diluted
  both (v1).

Point-in-time macros instead of a precomputed feature table
  One definition serves training, testing and live scoring, which rules
  out future leakage by construction.

Managing agents kept out of portfolio linking
  Precision over recall: merging unrelated owners through a shared
  management company would invent portfolios.

Exact-name linking for portfolios
  Simple, auditable, no false merges from fuzzy matching. Costs some recall
  (spelling variants across registrations stay split).

Writeback in a separate database file
  Rebuilding derived data must never destroy human work.

Manhattan only, 2020 onward
  Fits the 10-12 hour budget and keeps every step under a minute. The code
  is borough-agnostic apart from the download filters.

Streamlit for the app
  Fastest way to a working map, detail page and forms in Python. It stands
  in for what Foundry's Workshop would provide.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
23. LIMITATIONS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

- Outcomes are recorded violations, which reflect inspection patterns as
  well as building condition.
- Building attributes (owner, floors, units) and landlord links are today's
  values, also used in the backtest (mild look-ahead).
- HPD close dates are approximated by the current-status date.
- HPD violations reach BIN only when the lot holds one building (89.2%).
- Landlord linking uses exact names; spelling variants across registrations
  stay split. Only HPD-registered rentals (about 28k of 40.5k lots) have a
  portfolio.
- Managing-agent names are not normalised ("STELLAR MGMT" vs "STELLAR
  MANAGEMENT" count as different agents).
- Validation features for 2022 see only 24 of 36 months for 3-year windows.
- About 10k facade filings have no filing date (mostly "No Report Filed").
- Top-100 precision has about +/-5 points of sampling noise for hazards.
- Manhattan only; no 311 or FDNY data.

24. BUGS FOUND ALONG THE WAY, AND WHAT THEY TAUGHT
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

- Hand-set weights lost to baselines -> measure against naive baselines
  before trusting any score.
- Unlinked lots lumped into one NULL group in window functions, giving them
  a shared fake "owner rate" -> NULL keys need explicit handling in
  partitions.
- Queue selection survived a refresh while the table changed, so a second
  click would have tasked the wrong buildings -> reset widget state after
  actions that change the underlying list.
- Complaints appeared to stop in April 2024; the source was later refreshed
  -> check a feed's update date before concluding data is missing.
- A demo-script claim ("almost all in the top 2%") turned out to be 9 of 58
  -> verify every number before publishing it.
- "About a third of DOB violations are paperwork" was actually a fifth ->
  same lesson.
- Scoring took over 10 minutes because driver text was built row by row
  with iterrows -> vectorise, and skip work whose output is not used
  (now about 20 seconds).
- The basemap looked blank in previews; it was rendering but too pale to
  see in scaled screenshots -> check at full resolution before "fixing".
- The landing-page preview failed on port 5060, which Chromium blocks
  (SIP) -> use common dev ports.
- The Vercel CLI wrote an access token into the site folder -> exclude
  .env* explicitly from deployments and delete what is not needed.

26. INTERVIEW TALKING POINTS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

- Start with the user and decision (which buildings first, and why), not
  the model.
- Entity resolution was most of the work: three different keys, condo
  billing lots, placeholder BINs, and a measured match rate per source.
- The ontology makes non-obvious links visible: an LLC on paper becomes a
  59-building landlord whose other buildings are mostly high-risk.
- Point-in-time features make the backtest trustworthy by construction.
- Honest evaluation: the first version lost to baselines; a validation gain
  did not replicate; the test year was used once.
- Explainability was measured, not assumed: free for housing distress,
  costly for hazards.
- Writeback closes the loop: inspector outcomes become future labels.
- Three judgement calls worth explaining: why the lot is the scoring unit,
  why managing agents do not link landlords, and why there was no further
  tuning after the hazard result.
